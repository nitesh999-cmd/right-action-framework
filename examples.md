# Worked Examples

> **This does not tell you what the universe wants. It helps you think clearly before you act.**

Eight everyday decisions run through the Right Action Framework. These are illustrations of the *shape* of good output — not advice for your situation. Run your own decision through [`framework.md`](framework.md).

Each example follows: real decision → what's loud → right responsibility → right action → attachment/control → practical output.

---

## 1. The angry email decision

**Real decision:** Whether to send a sharp reply to a colleague who took credit for my work.

**What's loud:** Anger and pride. A bit of revenge ("they should be exposed").

**Right responsibility:** To represent my contribution accurately and keep working professionally — not to humiliate anyone.

**Right action:** Don't send the hot version. Send a calm, factual note (or raise it with my manager) that states what I did, with specifics.

- **Why:** A factual record protects my work; a furious email mostly discharges my anger and weakens my position.
- **Principle (theme only):** Self-restraint over harshness — Ch. 16; steadiness — Ch. 2.
- **Watch:** The urge to "win" the message.
- **Don't:** Send anything written while angry; CC people to embarrass them.
- **Can control:** My wording, my evidence, my timing.
- **Cannot control:** How they react or whether they admit it.
- **Next 3 steps:** (1) Draft the hot version and delete it. (2) Write a 3-line factual version. (3) Sleep on it; send tomorrow or take it to my manager.
- **Reminder:** State the facts clearly; let the credit sort itself out.

## 2. The business opportunity decision

**Real decision:** Whether to take on a large client that would stretch my capacity.

**What's loud:** Desire (revenue, status) and a little fear of missing out.

**Right responsibility:** To my existing clients, my standards, and my ability to deliver what I promise.

**Right action:** Define the conditions under which I'd say yes (scope, price, timeline, help), then accept or decline against those conditions.

- **Why:** A clear yes is built on whether I can deliver well, not on excitement.
- **Principle (theme only):** Right action matched to one's actual role/capacity — Ch. 18; act well without grasping at the reward — Ch. 3.
- **Watch:** FOMO dressed up as ambition.
- **Don't:** Say yes on a high; over-promise to win it.
- **Can control:** My terms, my honesty about capacity.
- **Cannot control:** Whether they accept my terms.
- **Next 3 steps:** (1) List delivery requirements. (2) Set non-negotiable terms. (3) Respond with a conditional yes or a clean no.
- **Reminder:** Win work you can actually deliver well.

## 3. The relationship conflict decision

**Real decision:** Whether to bring up a recurring hurt with my partner now or let it pass again.

**What's loud:** Avoidance and a quieter resentment building underneath.

**Right responsibility:** To be honest about what matters to me, kindly and clearly.

**Right action:** Raise it once, calmly, as a specific request — not as a list of grievances.

- **Why:** Repeated avoidance turns small hurts into resentment; one clear, kind conversation is the honest move.
- **Principle (theme only):** Patience and non-hatred in conduct — Ch. 12; courage to do the uncomfortable right thing — Ch. 2/3.
- **Watch:** Letting avoidance win, or swinging to attack.
- **Don't:** Ambush, keep score, or open with "you always…".
- **Can control:** My tone, timing, and one clear ask.
- **Cannot control:** Their response.
- **Next 3 steps:** (1) Name the one specific thing. (2) Pick a calm moment. (3) Make one concrete request.
- **Reminder:** Say the true thing kindly, once.

## 4. The money / risk decision

**Real decision:** Whether to put a meaningful chunk of savings into a risky bet a friend is excited about.

**What's loud:** Desire (quick upside) and social pressure.

**Right responsibility:** To protect my own financial stability and decide on the facts.

**Right action:** Slow down, separate the friendship from the decision, and only risk what I could fully lose without harm.

- **Why:** Excitement and loyalty are not analysis. (This is not financial advice — for real money, talk to a qualified adviser.)
- **Principle (theme only):** Steadiness and self-mastery over impulse — Ch. 2/6.
- **Watch:** Wanting to please my friend; fear of missing out.
- **Don't:** Decide on a high; risk money I actually need.
- **Can control:** How much, how fast, and who I consult.
- **Cannot control:** The market, the outcome.
- **Next 3 steps:** (1) Write what losing it all would mean. (2) Set a max amount (possibly zero). (3) Speak to a qualified adviser before committing.
- **Reminder:** Decide with a clear head; this is high-stakes — get proper advice.

## 5. The work overwhelm decision

**Real decision:** Everything feels urgent; what do I actually do next?

**What's loud:** Fear and a scattered, frozen feeling.

**Right responsibility:** To do my real work well, which means choosing — not doing everything at once.

**Right action:** Pick the one task that most reduces risk or unblocks others, and do only that next.

- **Why:** Overwhelm is a decision problem, not a volume problem; sequencing restores control.
- **Principle (theme only):** Disciplined, steady action over panic — Ch. 6; action over freezing — Ch. 3.
- **Watch:** Busywork that feels safe but moves nothing.
- **Don't:** Multitask out of anxiety; reorganise the list instead of acting.
- **Can control:** What I do in the next hour.
- **Cannot control:** The total size of the pile today.
- **Next 3 steps:** (1) Write the top 3. (2) Pick the one that unblocks the most. (3) Do it for one focused block.
- **Reminder:** One right thing at a time beats ten anxious ones.

## 6. The parenting decision

**Real decision:** Whether to push my teenager harder on a commitment they want to quit.

**What's loud:** Fear (for their future) and a bit of ego ("my kid doesn't quit").

**Right responsibility:** To guide and to listen — to help them learn, not to win the argument.

**Right action:** Have a calm conversation to understand why, set a fair expectation (e.g. finish the term, then choose), and let them own the choice within it.

- **Why:** Pushing from my fear teaches compliance; guiding teaches judgment.
- **Principle (theme only):** Patience and balanced conduct — Ch. 12; discern what is mine to do — Ch. 18.
- **Watch:** My ego, my fear about how it "looks".
- **Don't:** Make it a power struggle; decide before listening.
- **Can control:** How I listen, the boundary I set.
- **Cannot control:** What they ultimately love or choose.
- **Next 3 steps:** (1) Ask why, and actually listen. (2) Agree a fair checkpoint. (3) Let them choose within it.
- **Reminder:** Guide the child in front of me, not the image in my head.

## 7. The health / lifestyle discipline decision

**Real decision:** Whether to actually start (and keep) a basic exercise and sleep routine.

**What's loud:** Comfort-seeking and "I'll start properly Monday".

**Right responsibility:** To look after the one body I have, in small repeatable ways.

**Right action:** Choose the smallest version I'll actually repeat — and start today, not Monday.

- **Why:** Discipline is built from small steady actions, not from a perfect plan that never starts. (Not medical advice — check with a professional for health conditions.)
- **Principle (theme only):** Discipline and self-control — Ch. 6.
- **Watch:** Perfectionism that becomes an excuse.
- **Don't:** Design an elaborate plan; wait for motivation.
- **Can control:** Today's small action.
- **Cannot control:** Fast results.
- **Next 3 steps:** (1) Pick a 10-minute version. (2) Do it today. (3) Repeat tomorrow; track the streak, not the intensity.
- **Reminder:** Small and repeated beats big and someday.

## 8. Whether to act now or wait

**Real decision:** A reversible-ish move I keep hovering over — act now or wait for more certainty?

**What's loud:** A mix of impatience ("just do it") and fear ("not yet").

**Right responsibility:** To act on enough information, not perfect information, and to know which this is.

**Right action:** Decide what specific information would actually change my choice. If it's gettable soon and matters, wait briefly for it. If not, act.

- **Why:** Waiting only helps if the wait buys a decision-changing fact; otherwise it's just anxiety.
- **Principle (theme only):** Clarity over confusion, steadiness — Ch. 2; action over avoidance — Ch. 3.
- **Watch:** Calling avoidance "due diligence" — or rushing to escape discomfort.
- **Don't:** Wait for certainty that won't come; act just to stop the tension.
- **Can control:** What I learn next and when I decide.
- **Cannot control:** Whether the future cooperates.
- **Next 3 steps:** (1) Name the one fact that would change my mind. (2) See if it's gettable soon. (3) Set a decision date and keep it.
- **Reminder:** Wait for facts, not for comfort — then act.
